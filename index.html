A Citizens' Framework◆Victoria, BC · 2026◆v1.9.1 · July 30, 2026

A city that works.

Proven ideas. Measurable results.

What should we hope for our city? What do we have the right to demand — for ourselves and for our children?

The time for complaining is over. The time has come to imagine a city that works, want it with everything we have, and build it together — with realism and method.

A City That Works is an independent, non-partisan policy framework for the City of Victoria, drafted by residents who want practical results from their local government. It is 0% ideological, 100% pragmatic — its only loyalty is to what works elsewhere and can work here.

This is not a party. There are no whips, no candidate slate, no exclusivity — just a public menu of evidence-based municipal policies, costed and sourced, open for adoption by any candidate.

◆ A Foundation, Not a Pillar — Honouring the Host Nations

Victoria is built on the unceded traditional territories of the lək̓ʷəŋən-speaking peoples — today represented by the Songhees Nation and the Esquimalt Nation. Any serious program for the City must recognize this and build it into how the City operates, not just how it speaks.

◆ How this framework was built

0% ideology. 100% pragmatic.

We started where the truth is most visible: the budget. Politicians make promises; numbers don't lie. Victoria's operating budget grew 31.4% in three years, from $300.0M in 2023 to the $394.1M adopted for 2026, alongside $160.1M in capital — with VicPD's budget nearly doubling in a decade, from $50.6M to $86.8M requested for 2026, and no competitive benchmarking, AI, or smart infrastructure deployed. For every problem a Victorian faces daily, we applied the same six-step method.

01
Identify the root cause
Not the symptom — the structural reason the problem exists.
02
Assess what the City can actually do
Municipalities have specific powers. Don't promise what requires provincial or federal action.
03
Study what works elsewhere
Pittsburgh's Surtrac: −25% travel time, −21% emissions. Phoenix managed competition: $25M+ saved 1978–88. Calgary SAVE: $74M targeted, $60M found. We also study what doesn't translate — Helsinki, Singapore and Boston built city-scale digital twins, but Victoria is 7–25× smaller, so we start with the data layers they built first.
04
Measure with technology
Use data, sensors, and digital tools to understand before deciding.
05
Build solutions with experts
Engineers, police, builders, social workers, business owners, neighbourhood associations, residents.
06
Cost every measure
Direct and indirect impacts, costs and revenues. Every single proposal has a budget impact estimate.
◆ The 5-Step Process

No major new public project without these five steps.

The Dowler Place file proved why this matters. Under this framework, no project skips any step.

STEP 01
Local consultation
Problems, pain points, what needs to improve.
STEP 02
Expert design
Engineers, planners, affected residents.
STEP 03
Visualize & analyze
AI imagery (M53b) + GIS traffic, utility and infrastructure analysis (M53). See the effects on adjacent streets and businesses before a dollar is spent.
STEP 04
Referendum if > $25M
Binding public vote on new (non-lifecycle) capital, under the Community Charter — Assent Voting or the Alternative Approval Process.
STEP 05
Quarterly reports
On time, on budget, or explain why not.
◆ What delivery looks like

Victoria in 2030.

Everything below is the result of these measures being delivered. It is written in the present tense because that is the point of a plan.

It is a Tuesday evening in the spring of 2030 and Pandora Avenue is a street people walk down on purpose. The lights came on at dusk and they will stay on, dimmed but never off, until morning. They are warm and they suit the buildings. Nobody is watching you walk home, because we chose better lighting over cameras and wrote that choice into the framework so it could not quietly be reversed.

A bylaw officer goes past on a bicycle. It is after seven, and that used to be the hour the City stopped. The storefront beside you is occupied. So is the next one, and the one after that. Downtown vacancy is near 5%, and the shop owner has a name and a phone number at City Hall for when something goes wrong.

Bastion Square is a square again. Government Street closes to cars on summer weekends, and the businesses that were nervous about it in 2027 asked for it to be permanent in 2029, because the foot traffic data was published either way and it settled the argument. On the harbour you can now walk the whole shoreline without leaving the water's edge, from Songhees Point to Dallas Road, because the gaps got closed one at a time.

Your kids still live here. That is the part that matters most and the part that was least certain. Twelve thousand homes were approved and most of them got built, and enough were townhouses and three-bedroom units that a family could use one. There are streets in Fernwood and Oaklands that had nothing but detached houses in 2026 and now have four and six-unit buildings that look like they belong.

You got your tax notice in June and you understood it. There is one number on it that covers everything the City and the region bill you — property tax, water, sewer and stormwater together — with last year beside it and the next five years underneath. The residential rate went up by about inflation plus growth. Two years ago the City collected more than it needed and the difference came back as a credit on the notice rather than disappearing into a reserve, because a rule required it and six of nine councillors would have had to vote in public to keep it.

Every quarter the numbers are published. Permit times by category. Pothole report-to-fix. Graffiti removed inside 48 hours. Needles collected. Shelter spaces available at 5 PM. Debt outstanding and debt per household. Nobody filed a freedom-of-information request to learn any of it.

And in the middle of the city there is one thing that was built rather than fixed, chosen from three options by the people who live here and approved at a referendum before a shovel moved. It is the sort of place you take visitors.

None of this required more money than Victoria already spends. It required spending it on purpose, measuring the result, and publishing the result whether or not it flattered anyone. The 12 Commitments below are how you check whether we did it. That is the whole offer.

◆ Separation of Powers

What the City actually controls.

Before making promises, we must be honest about what the City of Victoria actually controls. Many of the problems residents face — healthcare, criminal sentencing, income support — fall under provincial or federal jurisdiction. We advocate aggressively to senior governments for action in their domains, but we never promise what isn't ours to deliver. See the full plain-language guide →

City of Victoria controls
Land use & zoning · building permits · property taxes · bylaws & enforcement · municipal policing (via VicPD Board) · parks & recreation · local roads & sidewalks · water & sewer · garbage collection · local planning · business licences · public art & heritage designation
Capital Regional District controls
Regional water supply & treatment · regional wastewater · solid-waste disposal · regional parks · regional housing. You pay for these through utility rates and requisitions, not the property tax rate — and Victoria's CRD directors are elected on the same ballot as council. M66d reports it; M70c governs Victoria's posture toward it.
Province of BC controls
Healthcare · K-12 education · social housing funding (BC Housing) · welfare & income assistance · courts & sentencing · major transit (BC Transit) · highways · building code · vacancy & speculation taxes
Federal government controls
Immigration · criminal law · employment insurance · Old Age Security · major infrastructure grants · Indigenous affairs (Crown-Nation relationship) · ports & harbours

A framework that discusses only the City and the senior governments leaves out a large and fast-growing share of what a household actually pays. That is why the regional level is on this diagram.

Honest about timing: these are steady-state figures at the end of a four-year implementation. Year-one savings are a fraction, and the Year 1 cap is the binding constraint rather than a comfortable one. The full line-by-line math is in the Savings & Revenue Analysis.

◆ Deliberately excluded from every figure above

Two gated conditional tiers — roughly $450–700K/yr (v1.8: the M20 AAA maintenance standard, end-of-trip facilities, and the M23b frequent-network stop standard) and $500–850K/yr (v1.9: M26b civilian crisis response, M28d community paramedicine, M80c Local Area Plan capacity). Each proceeds in Year 2 only if the M15, M65 and M68 savings are verified as tracking at the end of Year 1; the two clinical measures additionally require a confirmed provincial or health-authority cost-share partner, because municipally funding a clinical service outright is the downloading M70b exists to refuse.

Two capital items: the M64b City-owned seismic retrofit, uncosted pending inventory, and the M45c signature public-space project ($40–80M), referendum-gated under M74. Neither is quietly netted into the headroom.

These are commitments to spend savings that arrive, not savings that are hoped for. A framework that quietly spends its own projected savings before they arrive is doing exactly what it criticizes the current council for doing.

◆ The Tax Glide Path

≤ 5.0%
Year 3+ (target)
~3.5%
CPI + population growth

The cap is on the residential rate, not total revenue — a cap on the total would let a council deliver a 9% homeowner bill and describe it as 6.5%. Any increase above the cap requires a supermajority of Council, 6 of the 9 members (the mayor plus 8 councillors), with a public explanation on the record. The same 6-of-9 standard governs the M66b refund, the M66c debt rule, the M66d utility discipline and the M50b arts floor.

◆ Principles

What we are — and what we're not.

✓ This framework IS
  • Non-partisan and open
  • Costed and sourced
  • Measurable and quarterly-reported
  • Adoptable by any candidate, in whole or in part
  • Open to correction by anyone with expertise
✗ This framework is NOT
  • A political party
  • A candidate slate
  • Exclusively held by any one candidate
  • Final — every measure is open to refinement
  • A substitute for the work of the host Nations or senior governments
We are
  • →Govern like managers — measure, adjust, deliver
  • →Consult before building — and listen to the answer
  • →Visualize before constructing — AI imagery and GIS analysis, not demolition
  • →Protect the environment with results, not slogans
  • →Enforce the rules that exist — before inventing new ones
  • →Respect taxpayers — every dollar audited, every quarter reported
  • →Cost every promise — no measure without a budget line
  • →Publish one household bill, not just a tax rate
  • →Publish the rules for our own AI before we use it
We aren't
  • →We don't do ideology — we do results
  • →We don't make promises we can't measure
  • →We don't pick fights — we fix problems
  • →We don't spend first and justify later — taxpayers decide on big new projects
  • →We don't try things in real life when we can simulate them first
  • →We don't deploy public surveillance — no City cameras, no behavioural AI, better lighting instead
  • →We don't let a machine decide about a person — a named human decides, every time
