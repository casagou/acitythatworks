A Citizens' Framework◆Victoria, BC · 2026◆v1.10 · August 5, 2026

A city that works.

An independent, non-partisan, fully costed policy framework for Victoria's October 2026 election — free for any candidate to adopt.

What should we hope for our city? What do we have the right to demand — for ourselves and for our children?

The time for complaining is over. The time has come to imagine a city that works, want it with everything we have, and build it together — with realism and method.

Drafted by residents who want practical results from their local government, A City That Works is 0% ideological, 100% pragmatic — its only loyalty is to what works elsewhere and can work here.

This is not a party. There are no whips, no candidate slate, no exclusivity — just a public menu of evidence-based municipal policies, costed and sourced, open for adoption by any candidate.

◆ Start Here

Thirteen measures define this framework.

More than a hundred costed measures is a toolkit, not a reading assignment. These thirteen define the framework; every other measure supports one of them. Each carries a ⭐ beside its heading in Every Measure.

  1. 01Measure 65 — Zero-based budgeting. Every one of the City's 200+ programs justified from zero on a rotating cycle. The engine that pays for everything else on this page.
  2. 02Measures 66–66d — The Taxpayer Guarantee. A capped tax glide path, over-collection refunded to taxpayers rather than swept into reserves, a debt rule, and one published Household Bill.
  3. 03Measure 15 — Competitive testing for waste and street cleaning. In-house and private bids on the same specification, both directions; the cheaper competent option wins.
  4. 04Measure 7 — Published permit times, by category. A published target for every permit category, and your answer in writing.
  5. 05Measure 26 — Downtown Public Order Team. One integrated command for the blocks that need it most.
  6. 06Measure 27 — Bylaw enforcement 6 AM to 10 PM, 7 days a week. Disorder does not keep office hours; enforcement should not either.
  7. 07Measure 11 — Cut unsheltered homelessness by 50%. Housing-First with STEP throughput, capacity-first enforcement, and a published quarterly count.
  8. 08Measure 30 — Modern smart streetlighting. The Kelowna/Halifax model: LED, IoT, lit all night — and no cameras, no behavioural AI, no audio surveillance.
  9. 09Measures 71 + 73b — A downtown revival with an owner. Storefront vacancy from 11% to 5%, one Team Victoria office, one number, one flagship event.
  10. 10Measure 45c — One signature public-space project. A Pandora linear park or a continuous Inner Harbour promenade: shortlisted by residents, approved by referendum, delivered on time and on budget.
  11. 11Measures 77 + 78 — You vote on the big spends. A referendum for new capital over $25 million, and the 5-step process for every major project.
  12. 12Measure 79b — An Integrity Commissioner, anchoring the Integrity Package: lobbyist registry, FOI fee abolished, published mandate letters, and a council-pay rule.
  13. 13Measure 64b — Seismic and coastal resilience. The hazard Victoria actually faces, funded and scheduled.

If you read only one pillar, read A Well-Managed City. It pays for the other four.

◆ How this framework was built

0% ideology. 100% pragmatic.

We started where the truth is most visible: the budget. Politicians make promises; numbers don't lie. Victoria's operating budget grew 31.4% in three years, from $300.0M in 2023 to the $394.1M adopted for 2026, alongside $160.1M in 2026 capital improvements (both figures from the 2026–2030 Financial Plan adopted May 7, 2026) — with VicPD's budget nearly doubling in a decade, from $50.6M to $86.8M requested for 2026, and no competitive benchmarking, AI, or smart infrastructure deployed. For every problem a Victorian faces daily, we applied the same six-step method.

01
Identify the root cause
Not the symptom — the structural reason the problem exists.
02
Assess what the City can actually do
Municipalities have specific powers. Don't promise what requires provincial or federal action.
03
Study what works elsewhere
Pittsburgh's Surtrac: −25% travel time, −21% emissions. Phoenix managed competition: $25M+ saved 1978–88. Calgary SAVE: $74M targeted, $60M found. We also study what doesn't translate — Helsinki, Singapore and Boston built city-scale digital twins, but Victoria is 7–25× smaller, so we start with the data layers they built first.
04
Measure with technology
Use data, sensors, and digital tools to understand before deciding.
05
Build solutions with experts
Engineers, police, builders, social workers, business owners, neighbourhood associations, residents.
06
Cost every measure
Direct and indirect impacts, costs and revenues. Every single proposal has a budget impact estimate.

The method has a delivery half. Who owns each measure, what happens in the first 100 days, and what publishes every quarter are set out in Implementation & Governance.

◆ The 5-Step Process

No major new public project without these five steps.

The Dowler Place file proved why this matters. Under this framework, no project skips any step.

STEP 01
Local consultation
Problems, pain points, what needs to improve.
STEP 02
Expert design
Engineers, planners, affected residents.
STEP 03
Visualize & analyze
AI imagery (M53b) + GIS traffic, utility and infrastructure analysis (M53). See the effects on adjacent streets and businesses before a dollar is spent.
STEP 04
Referendum if > $25M
Binding public vote on new (non-lifecycle) capital, under the Community Charter — Assent Voting or the Alternative Approval Process.
STEP 05
Quarterly reports
On time, on budget, or explain why not.
◆ What delivery looks like

Victoria in 2030.

Everything below is the result of the measures in this framework being delivered. It is written in the present tense because that is the point of a plan.

Travellers already rank Victoria among the best small cities in the world. By 2030, it is also the best run. That is the change on offer. Not more money. Management.

It is a Saturday morning in June 2030 and you walk Pandora from Cook Street to the harbour. Four years ago this was the street Victoria was known for, for all the wrong reasons. Today it is a linear park. Trees, benches, kids on a playground, storefronts open on both sides. Nobody was pushed to the next block to make this happen. Housing came first, the province paid its share, and the City published the count every quarter until the unsheltered number fell by half. Then Victoria voted, on a ballot, to build the park. It opened on time and on budget because every invoice was public.

Where the park meets the water, you keep walking. The waterfront walkway runs further than it did, and the signs carry the lək̓ʷəŋən names first. The continuous Inner Harbour promenade, runner-up on the signature-project ballot, is next on the list. A city that delivers one project on time and on budget earns the mandate to build another.

None of this happened by luck. It happened because Victoria changed how it is run.

In 2030, Victoria is the city that refunds you when it over-collects your taxes. The credit lands on your notice, not in a reserve. That is Measure 66b, written so a later council cannot quietly reverse it. Tax increases stepped down each year until they matched inflation plus growth, and the only new debt is debt voters approved on a ballot.

Your permit answer comes in writing. A date, published targets, quarterly results. Builders stopped routing around Victoria, 12,000 homes were approved, and most of them got built.

Every capital project over $25 million goes to the voters. Crystal Pool proved it in 2025, and the signature ballot proved it again: residents say yes to good projects. They just want to be asked.

And the city works at street level. A bylaw officer answers at 9 PM. Graffiti is gone in 48 hours. The streets are lit all night. Downtown vacancy sits near 5%, and the storefronts that were dark in 2026 have tenants.

Every quarter, the dashboard publishes every number, green or red, no spin. You do not have to trust anyone. You can check.

Victoria’s budget grew 31% in three years, from $300 million to $394 million, and the city did not get 31% better. That gap is the mandate. Close it, and everything above is paid for without a single new tax. Every line here has a measure, a cost, and a date behind it. If the numbers do not move, you will know by 2028, not at the next election.

No council has ever offered Victoria that deal. This framework does. The 12 Commitments below are how you check whether we did it.

◆ Separation of Powers

What the City actually controls.

Before making promises, we must be honest about what the City of Victoria actually controls. Many of the problems residents face — healthcare, criminal sentencing, income support — fall under provincial or federal jurisdiction. We advocate aggressively to senior governments for action in their domains, but we never promise what isn't ours to deliver. See the full plain-language guide →

City of Victoria controls
Land use & zoning · building permits · property taxes · bylaws & enforcement · municipal policing (via VicPD Board) · parks & recreation · local roads & sidewalks · water & sewer · garbage collection · local planning · business licences · public art & heritage designation
Capital Regional District controls
Regional water supply & treatment · regional wastewater · solid-waste disposal · regional parks · regional housing. You pay for these through utility rates and requisitions, not the property tax rate — and Victoria's CRD directors are elected on the same ballot as council. M66d reports it; M70c governs Victoria's posture toward it.
Province of BC controls
Healthcare · K-12 education · social housing funding (BC Housing) · welfare & income assistance · courts & sentencing · major transit (BC Transit) · highways · building code · vacancy & speculation taxes
Federal government controls
Immigration · criminal law · employment insurance · Old Age Security · major infrastructure grants · Indigenous affairs (Crown-Nation relationship) · ports & harbours

A framework that discusses only the City and the senior governments leaves out a large and fast-growing share of what a household actually pays. That is why the regional level is on this diagram.

◆ Deliberately excluded from every figure above

Two gated conditional tiers — roughly $450–700K/yr (v1.8: the M20 AAA maintenance standard, end-of-trip facilities, and the M23b frequent-network stop standard) and $500–850K/yr (v1.9: M26b civilian crisis response, M28d community paramedicine, M80c Local Area Plan capacity). Each proceeds in Year 2 only if the M15, M65 and M68 savings are verified as tracking at the end of Year 1; the two clinical measures additionally require a confirmed provincial or health-authority cost-share partner, because municipally funding a clinical service outright is the downloading M70b exists to refuse.

Two capital items: the M64b City-owned seismic retrofit, uncosted pending inventory, and the M45c signature public-space project ($40–80M), referendum-gated under M74. Neither is quietly netted into the headroom.

These are commitments to spend savings that arrive, not savings that are hoped for. A framework that quietly spends its own projected savings before they arrive is doing exactly what it criticizes the current council for doing.

The cap is on the residential rate, not total revenue — a cap on the total would let a council deliver a 9% homeowner bill and describe it as 6.5%. ◆ Responses to common criticisms

The honest answers.

The questions we hear most — and the answers we stand by. More are answered on the full FAQ page.

"You're too pro-business / anti-environment"
This framework supports both business and the environment. Small businesses are the backbone of our community, and practical environmental policies work better than symbolic ones. The framework includes 50% GHG reduction by 2030, 5,000 new trees, 60% green mobility, and Climate Friendly Homes expansion, with published uptake numbers, which the current council has not provided. That is not anti-environment. It is results-oriented environmentalism.
"You're cutting services / austerity"
We are not cutting services. We are ensuring every dollar delivers value. Good management isn't austerity, it's responsibility. Every dollar saved through zero-based budgeting and competitive bidding gets redirected to frontline services: more bylaw officers, faster permits, cleaner streets, modern infrastructure.
"You don't care about homelessness"
Compassion without accountability helps no one. This framework commits to a 50% reduction in unsheltered homelessness through Housing-First, supportive housing, wraparound services, and relentless advocacy to the Province. But we also enforce the rules, because residents, businesses and vulnerable people themselves deserve safe, orderly public spaces. The phased implementation plan proves we understand you cannot enforce without capacity.
"You just want to privatize everything"
We want competitive testing for the services where cost and quality can actually be measured, which means waste collection and street cleaning. The test runs in both directions against the same specification, so the cheaper competent option wins whether that turns out to be the in-house crew or a contractor. Workers are protected with mandatory re-employment clauses. The City retains oversight and audits performance. The Phoenix "managed competition" model even lets City workers bid against private firms to keep services public when public delivery is competitive. This is not ideology. It is the same approach used in cities across Canada and the world, by governments of every political stripe.
"Referendums slow everything down"
Only new (non-lifecycle) capital projects over $25M go to referendum, within the BC statutory framework. Lifecycle maintenance is excluded so essential infrastructure work proceeds. That means a handful of major new-build decisions per term, not every speed bump and park bench. The Crystal Pool referendum (February 2025, 58.7% Yes) proved Victorians can make informed decisions on major spending. When the City spends big on new capital, residents should decide.
"You're going to put surveillance in our streetlights"
No. This framework explicitly <em>rejects</em> audio surveillance, behavioural AI, gunshot/scream detection, and any "directed brightness at suspect" functionality on public streetlights. The streetlight measure (Measure 30) is the Kelowna/Halifax model: LED conversion, IoT-connected platform for outage detection and brightness modulation, optional environmental sensors. The crime-reduction benefit of streetlighting itself is real and requires no surveillance: about 14% on the updated 2022 systematic-review evidence, revised down from 21% in the earlier review. We cite the lower, current number deliberately.
"How does this differ from a candidate platform?"
This is a framework, not a campaign. It is open for adoption by any candidate, in whole or in part. Multiple candidates can run on these ideas, and they should.
