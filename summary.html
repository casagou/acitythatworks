The Problem in 30 Seconds

◆ Where Victoria is today

Victoria's operating budget is up roughly 31% in three years — $300.0M adopted in 2023 to the $394.1M adopted for 2026, plus $160.1M in capital (City of Victoria). The 2026 budget opened at 13.3% in November, was cut to 7.2% in March, and was adopted at 7.28% on May 7 — and only after roughly $7M of late one-time cuts, including a cancelled $2M debt-reduction payment and a $2.8M parking-reserve contribution. Four consecutive years above inflation plus population growth, every one closed with one-time measures rather than structural savings.

And 7.28% is not what a homeowner paid. That is the increase in total tax revenue. Council distributed it at 9.34% residential and 4.78% business — about $323 more on an average home assessed at $1,015,000 (City of Victoria; Victoria News). In 2025 the split was 8.18% residential against 5.6% business. The gap widens every year by design, as council moves the business-to-residential ratio from 3.67:1 toward 3:1 by 2030. The headline number is not the number in your mailbox.

VicPD now costs $750 per resident — the highest in BC, and 5.06 of 2026's 7.28 points were policing. Missing Middle delivered 61 permits in 2024 against a target of 150. 48% of downtown businesses would leave if their lease expired. $25M spent on homelessness since 2023 — with no published outcome metrics.

And the part nobody campaigns on. The property tax rate is not what a household pays. On top of it sit water, sewer, solid waste, and a stormwater fee of $218.42 per property per year. The CRD's own five-year forecast for regional water rates is 7.6%, 9.4%, 10.9%, 12.3% and 12.6% through 2030 (Times Colonist, November 2025) — compounding to roughly 65% in five years, against a nearly $2 billion thirty-year capital program. A tax-only promise caps the number residents see and says nothing about the number they pay.

No competitive benchmarking. No AI. No digital twin. No smart infrastructure. No zero-based reviews. No published household bill.

Victoria does not need to spend more. It needs to spend smarter.

The Flagship Measures

🏠 Liveable

  • 12,000+ net new homes by 2030 with composition targets: 25% missing middle, 20% 3BR+, 15% non-market, 30% rental. Victoria is ahead of the provincial five-year target of 4,902 net new units on volume and behind on three-bedroom and below-market. Composition is the problem, not volume
  • A pre-approved pattern book for duplex, triplex, fourplex, townhouse and laneway forms, with an automatic fast lane for anything built from it. Missing Middle produced 61 permits in 2024 against a target of 150, and the binding constraint is no longer zoning — it is cost and delay
  • Legalize the suites that already exist — a 24-month, life-safety-only amnesty with a named permitting concierge, no retroactive penalty, and no rent reset for the sitting tenant. Rental homes at zero land cost and zero construction
  • City land bank — strategic acquisition and competitive disposition of City-owned land (continuing Alto 2022)
  • Published permit times by category: simple <6 weeks, complex <6 months, rezoning <9 months
  • Adaptive traffic signals on major corridors (Pittsburgh evidence: −25% travel time, −21% emissions)
  • Champion Westshore rapid transit and the E&N corridor — endorse LRT/BRT, protect right-of-way
  • First-hour free parking in commercial areas + real-time parking availability
  • STEP-style supportive-housing throughput with BC Housing + Island Health + Pacifica — free up the supportive units, not just add to them
  • Short-term rental licensing tightening to protect the long-term rental pool
  • Reach Victoria's young adults (18–25) — youth-priority space, late-evening hours, post-secondary partnerships
  • Universal accessibility audit and remediation — every sidewalk, public building, washroom, transit-adjacent site
  • A walking city — published sidewalk gap inventory, annual kilometres closed, a crossing-spacing standard, and pedestrian signal timing at real walking speeds
  • Transit: what the City controls and will fund — a named transit priority capital line for bus lanes, queue jumps and signal priority, plus a published City position and a record of Commission decisions. No promises about routes and fares, which are not ours
  • A standing agreement with School District 61 — joint use of gyms, fields and playgrounds, safe routes to school as a program, childcare on school sites

🛡️ Safe

  • Downtown Public Order Team (DPOT) — unified VicPD + Bylaw + Sanitation + Outreach, single daily briefing
  • Bylaw enforcement 6 AM – 10 PM, 7 days a week (vs. current 7 AM – 4 PM)
  • Smart LED streetlights — Kelowna/Halifax model. No audio surveillance. No behavioural AI. No City cameras. Self-financing in 3–4 years
  • Civilian crisis response — the right responder for the call, gated on a confirmed health-authority cost-share partner
  • Community paramedicine — reduce the calls, not just the response time
  • A voluntary business camera registry — VicPD-led, and the City owns no cameras and holds no recordings
  • Resolve the VicPD funding dispute with Esquimalt — transparent service-consumption formula, binding mediation if needed
  • Greater Victoria Regional Policing Services Review — 18-month co-sponsored review with binding mediation outcome
  • Phased capacity-first enforcement: enforce when there is somewhere to send people, not when there isn't
  • Drug-use buffer zones around schools, playgrounds, libraries and community centres
  • Reclaim the priority public spaces — permanent visible presence in the Inner Harbour, the Pandora corridor, Centennial Square and Beacon Hill Park, with a same-day protocol for structures blocking sidewalks and doorways. Humane, documented and predictable, with property stored for retrieval
  • Relentless with the dangerous, compassionate with the vulnerable — focused deterrence on trafficking, weapons and violent repeat offenders, coordinated with Crown counsel, built on the data Coun. Hammond's FOI surfaced: 24% of all 2022 police calls came from 19 addresses. Every enforcement action is published quarterly, including how much of it survived adjudication

🌳 Beautiful

  • Modernize underground utility GIS (M53) — coordinate excavation, reduce re-trenched streets, federal modernization-grant eligible
  • One-time LIDAR scan of the downtown core (M53c) — heritage protection, emergency-management baseline, planning-support data layer
  • AI-generated public consultation imagery (M53b) — continuing Coun. Dell's Beacon Hill precedent, on every meaningful public project
  • Heritage protection + restored street furniture — Victoria's character is its competitive advantage
  • Cultural-venue preservation tool — right-of-first-refusal plus a lease-bridge fund for designated live-music and arts spaces
  • Pedestrianization pilots — Bastion Square full, Government Street seasonal
  • One signature public-space project, chosen by residents — three costed concepts shortlisted (a Pandora corridor linear park, a continuous Inner Harbour promenade, a full Centennial Square renewal), all three visualized before the vote, decided by referendum, with a senior-government cost-share secured first. Sequenced after the safety and capacity work, not instead of it
  • Royal Athletic Park restoration — reverse the 2025 reserve raid and complete deferred upgrades (continuing Alto 2022)
  • 5,000 new trees by 2030, and a published canopy target of 35% by 2035 — a planting count measures effort, a canopy percentage measures result. Free seedlings for homeowners, because 75% of Victoria's canopy sits on private land
  • −50% community GHG by 2030 (19 points still needed from the current −31%)
  • Maximum-temperature rental bylaw (26°C) in at least one room during declared heat-warning periods
  • An arts and culture funding floor — published, set at the current level, reducible only by supermajority with a published rationale. Fiscal discipline paid for by the cultural sector is not discipline
  • A published municipal AI standard — public register, human-in-the-loop mandatory, automated adverse decisions prohibited, all AI-generated imagery labelled, prompts and outputs as FIPPA records. No comparable BC municipality has one
  • Seismic and coastal resilience — the hazard Victoria actually faces

💰 Well-Managed

  • Competitive testing for waste and street cleaning — roughly $2.4M/year, run in both directions against the same specification so City crews bid alongside private firms and the cheaper competent option wins either way. Workers protected by mandatory re-employment clauses (Phoenix precedent: $25M+ saved 1978–1988)
  • Zero-based budget review of all 200+ programs on a rotating cycle (Calgary SAVE: $74M targeted by 2022; $60M actually found per March 2026)
  • Tax glide path, on the number you actually pay: Year 1 ≤6.5%, Year 2 ≤5%, Year 3+ ≈CPI + population growth (3.5%) — capped on the residential rate, not on total revenue. Measured against the 9.34% residential increase council levied in 2026, not the 7.28% aggregate and not the 13.3% opening draft. A cap on the total would let a council send homeowners a 9% bill and call it 6.5%; this one cannot. Paired with a rule that the business-to-residential ratio is not moved to manufacture the number, and honesty that an individual bill still moves with BC Assessment's valuation. Year 1's differentiator is also the method: ≤6.5% without cancelling the debt-reduction payment, raiding the parking reserve, or deferring capital
  • Return over-collection to taxpayers — a credit on the notice rather than a sweep into reserves, overridable only 6 of 9 in public
  • A debt rule — stop borrowing against the next council
  • Vacant storefront strategy: advocate to the Province for enabling legislation, use existing levers (licensing, façade conditions, pop-up facilitation), publish a quarterly transparency report
  • Confront provincial downloading — quarterly public ledger of unfunded mandates plus a formal refusal threshold
  • Rationalize City real estate — sell or lease underused properties; proceeds to property-related debt and capital acquisition, per Community Charter s.188(2)(e)
  • The Household Bill — one published number covering property tax plus water, sewer, solid waste and stormwater, with a five-year forward view, published beside the glide path and never apart from it. Supermajority discipline on City-controlled utility increases above CPI plus growth
  • The regional bill — a published annual City position on CRD cost, a published record of Victoria representatives' votes, and staged approval of the $1.07 billion Goldstream filtration plant with a published business case, an independent alternatives analysis, and a rate impact stated in household dollars
  • Protect employment lands — no net loss of employment-designated land without a published replacement plan; marine-industrial land as a distinct protected category
  • A published Strategic Economic Plan and a simplification charter — every new rule earns its place, every old rule gets reviewed

🗳️ Democratic

  • Referendum on every new (non-lifecycle) capital project over $25M — within the BC Local Government Act and Community Charter framework (Assent Voting or the Alternative Approval Process)
  • Finish the Saanich amalgamation file — the Citizens' Assembly already sat (48 randomly selected residents, eight sessions, September 2024 to April 2025) and recommended amalgamation on April 5, 2025. Commission the financial, transitional and service-impact analysis the Province required in April 2026, begin the First Nations consultation it named, then return and request a binding referendum. Do not re-run the Assembly
  • Integrity Commissioner office plus an MNP governance report implementation tracker
  • A public lobbyist registry for development files, and no fee to ask a question — BC's provincial registry does not cover municipalities. Meetings between development proponents and Council or staff are filed by the City within 10 business days, with no exemption for the mayor. The $10 freedom-of-information application fee is waived
  • Council pay changes take effect only after the next election — the independent review still happens; the councillors who vote on it are simply not the ones who receive it
  • The material change rule — no substantive change after the hearing without renotification
  • Quarterly performance dashboard — permits, potholes, crime, housing, budget variance — published, no spin
  • Government-to-government working tables with the Songhees Nation and the Esquimalt Nation, on a standing quarterly cadence
  • Bring back Local Area Plans — two neighbourhood plans a year on a published schedule, with parcel-level clarity a resident or a small builder can read without hiring a consultant, and infrastructure capacity stated in each one. Housing capacity is not reduced by this, and the alternative of repealing the 2025 OCP would consume a term and stall supply
  • Turnout and ballot access — 2022 turnout was roughly 38%. At least one weekend advance voting day (all four 2026 advance days are weekdays), voting places weighted toward the lowest-turnout neighbourhoods, campus and multi-unit voting, a turnout report by neighbourhood within 90 days, and election bylaws set 12 months out so no council writes the rules of its own re-election

Year one is a fraction of steady state, and the glide path is honest about that. Net fiscal headroom after every operating cost this framework creates is $4.3–14.0M per year on central assumptions, and approximately −$0.3M on pessimistic ones, pairing low savings and revenue against high costs. The pessimistic figure is published deliberately: on central assumptions the glide path is funded with room to spare, and on pessimistic ones it is funded by nothing and Year 1 slips. Six cost-bearing commitments, worth roughly $0.95–1.55M a year, are gated on verified Year 1 savings rather than booked — because a framework that spends its own projected savings before they arrive is doing what it criticizes the current council for doing. Full line-item math in the Savings & Revenue Analysis.

How Every Major New Project Gets Decided

  1. Consult locally — what residents say needs to improve
  2. Design with experts — engineers, planners, affected residents
  3. Visualize and analyze impact — AI imagery (M53b) and GIS analysis (M53) show the future before construction
  4. Referendum if >$25M (new, non-lifecycle capital), under BC statutory process
  5. Report quarterly during construction — on time, on budget, or explain why not

◆ Election day
The Victoria municipal election is on October 17, 2026. The ballot also elects 3 CRD directors and School District 61 trustees, and carries the non-binding question "Do you support Saanich and Victoria becoming one municipality?" Between now and then, every candidate should be able to answer one question clearly: "What works elsewhere that you would bring to Victoria?"
