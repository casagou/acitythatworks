Where each candidate stands.

55 topics · 210 scored marks · 15 candidates. Every mark is the output of two questions asked in order, so the same evidence produces the same mark regardless of who reads it. A grade is computed, not assigned — and every number on this page can be recomputed from the grid below.

The scale measures distance from this framework, not quality. A low grade means a candidate proposes something different, or has not yet said how. It does not mean they are wrong.

◆ Read this before the grades

Read the “graded on” column before the grade. A grade computed on 6 topics and a grade computed on 25 are not comparable claims. Silence is never scored, so a narrow base is a fact about a publication calendar, not about a candidate.

Most municipal candidates publish platform detail in September. A matrix scored in July and August therefore systematically under-reports challengers relative to incumbents, who have four years of votes on the record. That is a property of the calendar, not a finding about anyone, and it is the single most important caveat on this page. A re-score after nominations close on 11 September is mandatory before this document informs anything.

This page discloses four defects in its own scale, one of them now fixed at source, and prints no letter at all where the base is thinner than three topics. Read the defects before quoting any cell.

How a mark is decided

A mark requires a citable public source: a recorded council vote, a published platform, or a direct quotation in a named outlet. It is not a judgement about whether a candidate is good. It is the answer to two questions, asked in order.

Question 1 — does the candidate share the goal? Commits states an action they will take · Agrees names it as a goal or priority but states no action · Mentions raises the topic, states no goal · Disagrees a recorded vote against, or an explicit statement of opposition.

Question 2 — how would they do it? Same instrument names the mechanism this framework proposes · Comparable a different mechanism doing substantially the same work · A target, no instrument no mechanism but a number, a date or a dollar figure · Neither.

A C is not a bad grade. It means the candidate has said something concrete without naming an instrument, which is the ordinary state of a municipal platform in August. Below three scored topics no letter is shown. Ambiguity resolves upward.

One sentence, one topic. Where a quotation could be filed under several measures it is scored once, in the topic whose stated purpose it matches, and cross-referenced elsewhere. Position and delivery are separated: a published plank is a position and is scored as one, including a 2022 plank. Whether it was delivered is a separate Record line and is never scored.

What counts as a source

Every non-excluded cell requires a citable public source. The following are not sources and may not produce a score:

  1. Frame or tone alignment. “Results over ideology” is not a zero-based budgeting commitment.
  2. A stated lane. Caring about safety is not a position on bylaw enforcement hours.
  3. Bloc membership. Voting with a bloc on other files does not establish a position on this one.
  4. Third-party endorsement. An organization's policy is that organization's, not the candidate's.
  5. Professional background or a role held. A trades background is not a permit-reform position. A board seat, a committee chairmanship or a ministry post is not a position.
  6. Absence of action. An incumbent who did not do something has not opposed it. That is Record, not Opposed.
  7. Adjacency. A position on a related measure is not a position on this measure.

The test applied to every cell: could this score be defended to the candidate's face, with the source in hand? If not, it is excluded.

Coverage and selection. A scorecard that picks its own topics can produce almost any result without changing a single score, and the bias is invisible unless the selection rule is published. The rule: a measure gets a topic if at least one candidate has a locatable public position on it, so there is something to compare. Sub-measures are scored inside their parent's topic. Nothing is excluded because of who it would help or hurt. Coverage is 55 topics reaching all 131 measures of Program v1.9.1.

Which direction the omissions ran, checked rather than asserted. The clusters missing from the earlier topic set were families and schools, cleanliness, culture and sport, accessibility, parks, economic development and cost of living — exactly the areas where sitting councillors have operational records and where business-background challengers would score. Adding them produced new aligned marks for sitting councillors. The old selection understated the field, not this framework. The same held in v3.0: 21 new marks, 14 of them to two challengers with no relationship to the framework.

Four defects in the scale, quantified

These are properties of the methodology, not of the evidence. They are published here because a grade with a known defect that its publisher does not disclose is worse than no grade at all. One has now been fixed at source; three have not.

Fixed in v3.2 · 2 August 2026

1. Opposed at −2.0 was arithmetically asymmetric

The positive range runs 0.5 to 3.0; the negative value was −2.0, putting one contrary vote 2.5 points below the nearest positive mark. Because a grade is a mean, on a ten-topic column that is a quarter point of mean — a full grade band decided by a single vote, and a grade a single vote can decide is a grade an opponent destroys in one sentence. All four weightings were costed on the real data before one was chosen.

Why B rather than the others. Option A lets one 2023 vote outweigh ten considered positions, which measures the scale rather than the candidate. Option C sets a stated opposition equal in weight to no opposition at all, discarding real information a voter is entitled to. Option D removes the votes from the calculation entirely, and a scorecard that deletes the evidence it finds inconvenient has no claim on anyone's trust. B keeps every vote counted, keeps it negative, and stops it deciding a grade by itself. Opposed is −1.0 everywhere on this page.
The conflict of interest on this change, stated plainly. Five candidates hold an Opposed mark. Two of them, Gardiner and Hammond, are incumbents this document's author works with, and softening the penalty helps them. That is a genuine appearance problem and it does not disappear by being unintentional. What limits it: none of the four coordinated independents the author supports holds an Opposed mark anywhere in the matrix, so the change is worth nothing to them. Of the two candidates who actually crossed a band, one is Susan Kim, a progressive councillor with no relationship to this framework. The top five positions are identical under the old weighting and the new one.
