What this is

A side-by-side comparison of the Victoria 2026 mayoral and council field, scored against the A City That Works framework (~121 commitments across 5 pillars + 1 foundation, including sub-measures added through v1.1 to v1.8). Read it like a product spec sheet: each row is a policy area, each column is a candidate or candidate bloc, and the scoring tells you at a glance who supports what — and where ACTW is alone in proposing a specific mechanism.Who is in this comparison. All 9 sitting council members (Mayor Alto + 8 councillors), all declared 2026 candidates, and notable non-runners briefly marked. As of July 29, 2026 — 80 days out, and 34 days before nominations close on September 11:

  • 6 incumbents formally launched: Alto (May 20), Caradonna (Dec 21, 2025), Dell (Dec 31, 2025), Gardiner, Thompson, Hammond (July 6)
  • 2 incumbents now listed as declared: Kim and Loughton both appear on Livable CRD's confirmed-candidate list, which by its own stated policy admits a name only after a public announcement. No launch coverage in a major outlet has been located for either. Verify directly before relying on this.
  • 1 incumbent still undeclared: Coleman. He said in January that an incumbent's "electoral runway" should be about 90 days, "so, about mid-July." That window has now closed and he is absent from the confirmed list.
  • 10 declared challengers: Cseszko (May 12), Bowkett, Rothe, McInnis, Lee and Sandor (both June 2026), Garcia, Gibbs, Girard, Haley (dates unconfirmed)
  • 1 non-runner, 1 silent: Manak (declined April 15), Andrew (no 2026 signal)

The single most consequential fact about this field: Alto is still the only declared mayoral candidate. Eighty days from the vote and 34 days from the nomination deadline, no one has entered the mayoral race against her. Del Manak's April withdrawal left that lane empty and it has stayed empty. Note on the declared challengers: Where a candidate has not yet published a position on a given measure, the cell is marked ⚪ silent rather than guessed. Silence is not opposition, and scoring is updated as published platform detail becomes available. Why this exists. A voter, journalist, or rival candidate should be able to scan one page and see: (1) what ACTW proposes on each major issue, (2) where each candidate has publicly committed, dissented, or stayed silent, and (3) where ACTW proposes a specific mechanism that no other candidate on the field has matched. What this is not. Not a slate. Not an attack piece. Not a complete portrait of any candidate — single-line ideas inevitably flatten nuance. Use this as the starting point, not the final word.

How to Read This

Scoring legend

Aligned — a public source in which the candidate names the same mechanism as ACTW, or a substantively equivalent one

Partial — a public source on the same policy area, but a different, weaker, or unspecified mechanism

Opposed — a recorded vote against, or an explicit public statement of opposition. Nothing else qualifies

No public position — nothing located. Not opposition, not agreement

Record — a sourced factual statement about what happened, or did not happen, during a term in office. This is not a score and never appears as ❌. Introduced in v1.8 because the previous version conflated the two: "no ZBB during three years in office" is a record fact about the Alto council, not a position Mayor Alto has stated. An incumbent who never acted on a measure has not opposed it, and a matrix that marks them opposed is making a claim it cannot source.

Sourcing standard — the written evidence rule

Every non-⚪ cell requires a citable public source: a recorded council vote, a published platform document or campaign website, or a direct quotation in a named outlet. The following are not sources and may not produce a score:

  1. Frame or tone alignment. That a candidate's language sounds compatible with ACTW is not a position on an ACTW mechanism. "Results over ideology" is not a zero-based budgeting commitment.
  2. A stated "lane." Caring about safety is not a position on bylaw enforcement hours.
  3. Bloc membership. That a councillor votes with a bloc on other files does not establish their position on this one.
  4. Third-party endorsement. An organization's policy is that organization's, not the candidate's, unless the candidate has adopted it publicly.
  5. Professional background. A trades background is not a permit-reform position. A ministry role is not a downloading position.
  6. Absence of action. An incumbent who did not do something has not opposed it. That is 📋 Record, not ❌.
  7. Adjacency. A position on a related measure is not a position on this measure.

Where reporting is contradictory, the most recent on-the-record source wins and the conflict is flagged. The test applied to every cell: could this score be defended to the candidate's face, with the source in hand? If not, it is ⚪.

The scoring standard

Every score has to survive one test: could it be defended to the candidate’s face, with the source in hand? If not, the cell is ⚪.

  • A named mechanism, not a frame. Agreeing with the goal is not a position on the measure. “Safer streets” is a value; “bylaw enforcement to 10 PM, seven days, funded from competitive-testing savings” is a position. Only the second is scorable.
  • Adjacency is not a source. A quote about one policy area does not become a position on a neighbouring one, and a bloc’s general disposition is never evidence of an individual’s view.
  • A record is not a stated position. Where the sourced fact is what a council did rather than what a candidate has said they would do, the cell is 📋, not ✅ or ❌.
  • ⚪ is the default. Most candidates have not published a position on most measures. That is the ordinary state of a municipal campaign, not a mark against anyone.

Coverage and Selection — which measures are scored, and which are not

Why this section exists. A scorecard that picks its own topics can produce almost any result it wants without changing a single score. Choose nine topics well and you can make any candidate look prepared or unprepared. That is the most common defect in candidate scorecards and it is invisible unless the selection rule is published. So here is ours, with the arithmetic. The selection rule, stated. A measure gets a topic table if at least one candidate has a locatable public position on it, so there is something to compare. A measure where the entire field is ⚪ gets a row in Where ACTW Stands Alone instead, because a nineteen-row table of ⚪ communicates nothing a single line cannot. Sub-measures are scored inside their parent measure's table rather than given their own. Nothing is excluded because of who it would help or hurt. The arithmetic. 30 topic tables reaching roughly 90 of the framework's ~121 measures, about 74%. Version 1.8 of this matrix had 20 tables reaching about 37%, and the ten tables added in v1.9 were chosen specifically to close the largest gaps rather than the most flattering ones. What is still not scored, and why. M2–M5 are folded into the M1 Foundation table. M8, M10, M31b, M64b, M66c and M10 are all-⚪ and sit in Where ACTW Stands Alone. M28, M28b, M31–M37, M44–M49, M54–M57, M59–M64, M68–M70, M74, M79 and M79c are reached only through a parent table or a candidate profile. That remaining ~26% is a genuine gap and it is named here rather than left for someone else to find.

Which direction the omissions ran, checked rather than asserted. The obvious worry is that the topic selection favoured the author's allies. It did not, and it may have run the other way. The clusters missing from v1.8 were families and schools, cleanliness, culture and sport, accessibility, parks, economic development, and cost of living. Those are exactly the areas where sitting councillors have operational records (Alto on arts, parks and childcare; Dell on Hermann's, BreakOut West and OUR DWTN; Kim on public washrooms and multilingual access) and where business-background challengers would score (Bowkett, Cseszko, McInnis on downtown and basics). Adding them in v1.9 produced two new ✅ marks, both to sitting progressive councillors — Kim on M24b public washrooms and Dell on cultural-venue and event mechanisms — and raised 🟡 counts for Alto, Bowkett, Rothe and McInnis. The old selection understated the field, not ACTW.

Where the ✅ marks actually fall, per capita. Across all topic tables the progressive bloc averages roughly 3.4 ✅ per member and the fiscal-restraint bloc roughly 3.0. That near-parity holds only because tenant protections, the heat bylaw, climate and provincial downloading are scored, and four progressives hold ✅ on each. Had those topics been omitted the document would be indefensible. They are in, and they stay in. The one asymmetry that is real and deliberate. The Current Administration section gives Mayor Alto four record tables that no other candidate receives, and her profile carries a contested-items subsection that no other profile carries. That is a considered choice, not an oversight: she is the only candidate with a mayoral record, and a framework whose central claim is that the last four years lacked method has to show the four years. It is stated as a choice so a reader can discount it if they disagree. Every item in those tables is 📋 Record, not ❌ Opposed.

Platform Status — what ⚪ actually means for each candidate

This table exists because ⚪ was doing three different jobs and hiding the difference. "No public position located" can mean a candidate published a detailed platform that does not cover the measure, or published positioning language with detail still to come, or published nothing anyone can find. Those are three different states of knowledge and the matrix was flattening them into one symbol. Read every ⚪ in this document against the row below.

The structural asymmetry, stated plainly. Most municipal candidates publish platform detail in September. Nominations close September 11 and the campaign period begins September 19. A matrix scored in July therefore systematically under-reports challengers relative to incumbents, because incumbents have four years of votes on the record and challengers have a launch statement. That is a property of the calendar, not a finding about anyone, and it is the single most important caveat on this page. Every challenger's score should be expected to rise between now and late September, and a re-score after September 11 is mandatory before this document informs anything.

Methodology + Caveats

Scope and time-stamp. Current as of July 30, 2026 — 79 days before the October 17, 2026 election. Nominations open September 1 and close September 11 at 4 p.m.; the declaration of candidates follows on September 11. The pre-campaign period began July 20, 2026, so Elections BC's third-party advertising rules under LECFA are now live. The campaign period begins September 19. Positions will shift, and the field is not final until September 11.

What is incomplete in this version, stated plainly. Four of the sixteen declared council candidates — Garcia, Gibbs, Girard, and Haley — have no locatable public policy record of any kind. They are included and scored silent, but this matrix should not be described as a complete portrait of the field until they are researched directly. Kim's and Loughton's declared status rests on a single volunteer-maintained source and needs independent confirmation. Coleman's intentions are unknown past his own stated mid-July window. And the largest incompleteness is structural rather than a research gap: most candidates publish platform detail in September, so a July scoring systematically under-reports challengers against incumbents who have four years of votes on the record. Every challenger's score should be expected to rise. A re-score after nominations close on September 11 is mandatory before this document informs anything.

Scoring approach. Each cell reflects the most recent public position found in: voting record (City of Victoria council minutes, major-outlet reporting), declared platform (campaign websites, launch statements), or direct public statement (interview, social media, press). Where reporting is contradictory, the most recent on-the-record source wins. Why ⚪ silent is not ❌ opposed, and what it does mean. A candidate not having taken a public position on a measure is not the same as opposing it. Treating silence as opposition would be a false negative. But ⚪ is not one thing, and the Platform Status table above sets out which of three states it represents for each candidate: a published platform that does not cover the measure, published positioning with detail still pending, or nothing locatable. Read every ⚪ against that table. Where the silence persists into the campaign period, this matrix will be updated. Why this matrix is ACTW-anchored, not neutral. The structure is candid: rows are ACTW measures and scoring is against ACTW positions. Two reasons. First, ACTW is the only platform in the field with roughly 121 specific costed commitments, and there is no other framework with that density to anchor against. Second, the explicit purpose is to show where ACTW stands alone on a mechanism. What makes an anchored comparison defensible rather than self-serving is that the selection rule is published, the coverage arithmetic is published, the direction the omissions ran is checked rather than asserted, and there is a section listing what the field has that ACTW does not. All four are now on this page.
